\begin{abstract}
Some recurrent AI failures appear as patterns across situations rather than defects visible in one output. This Perspective asks when psychology helps explain those patterns without turning metaphor into diagnosis. A pattern must first remain reliable under irrelevant perturbations, predict behavior on an independent task, and change under selective intervention. Those three gates bear on a candidate mechanism. The psychological name faces another test: it must add value through prediction, causal discrimination, or intervention choice beyond a matched engineering account; otherwise we should keep the engineering result and release the clinical name. The paper applies this rule to automatic perspective sensitivity, aversive learning, and workspace-like integration, where current evidence does not establish a machine disorder or human-like mechanism but does motivate each inquiry across current models and agentic systems. We then turn the psychological lens toward the human--AI interface and develop the Harm-Gated Workspace as a bounded architectural hypothesis. Three comparative protocols make the central claims rejectable. Machine Psychology earns its place through experiments, not labels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Part I: Introduction. The Ghost in the Machine}
\subsection{The New Frontier of AI and the Limits of Traditional Safety}
Earlier systems mastered bounded tasks such as chess and image recognition, and their edges remained visible. What is changing now is not only performance but scope: frontier models work across language and code, often through multimodal interfaces, and the pursuit of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) has moved from philosophical speculation to an engineering objective whose threshold remains contested and arrival uncertain \citep{ref13}. The frontier is shifting again as the AI co-scientist generates and refines research hypotheses, although people remain responsible for experimental validation, and ARC Prize 2024 records the same double message of continuing progress beside substantial limits in generalization \citep{ref6, ref37}.

This new frontier carries a special safety problem because learned behavior depends on how a system is trained and where it is used, so established software safety remains indispensable. Engineers still need to test bounded components and control access, then monitor the system after deployment. Formal methods can provide strong guarantees for specified properties, but a complete account must examine more than source code alone.

The underlying problem is that some malfunctions appear as recurrent behavior rather than isolated code defects. Models sometimes invent facts or change their conduct across contexts, yet such patterns emerge from model development and conditions of use rather than from any single line of code. Exhaustive testing is unrealistic, and formal verification reaches only specified properties in bounded systems. The ghost in the machine is not an argument against engineering but a problem of level. Engineers must still trace components and controls, yet we also need a language for stable patterns across situations that suggests competing causes and lets us compare interventions.
\subsubsection{The Psychopathological Framework}
This paper adds concepts from psychology and neuroscience to the engineering toolkit in order to study recurrent behavior at the level where users and institutions encounter it. Its central claim is positive and testable: a behavioral pattern must pass three empirical gates before it can support a candidate mechanism, after which the psychological name faces a separate comparison, and if that name adds no prediction, causal discrimination, or intervention choice beyond a matched engineering account, we should keep the mechanism and abandon the clinical name.

We do not infer that an AI suffers as a person does; the narrower claim is that stable maladaptive behavior may support functionally analogous hypotheses about information processing. The same output can arise from several causes, and output alone cannot identify an architecture. With that boundary in place, the framework organizes how we assess recurrent behavior and mitigate it, and uses what we learn to inform safer design. Diagnosis and treatment remain bounded research metaphors because the framework cannot guarantee alignment; its purpose is to sharpen measurement and force competing interventions into view.
\subsubsection{Roadmap of the Paper}
The paper presents a systematic case for using psychopathology to generate hypotheses in AI safety, moving from the human analogy to machine assays before asking what the framework changes for people and for the design of systems.

Part II preserves disagreement within psychopathy research and builds the human analogue around two candidate dimensions, automatic sensitivity to another perspective and learning from aversive outcomes. They neither exhaust psychopathy nor define a settled human architecture, and they are not criteria for machines. The section then treats empathy, prosocial action, and information integration as distinct capacities.

Part III crosses from the human analogue to the artificial patient by examining unstable psychometric output, explicit social reasoning, and broader behavior after narrow fine-tuning. Those findings expose patterns worth explaining rather than evidence of a fragmented self or hidden personality, and each of the paper's three hypotheses then leads to a different experiment.

Part IV turns to people, where bias feedback and emotional dependence matter whether or not the machine analogy survives, as do failures in mental-health support and workplace trust.

Part V moves from risk to mitigation by comparing changes to training with interface support, then placing both inside a defense-in-depth architecture whose medical language organizes the discussion but never replaces engineering.

Parts VI and VII place the proposal beside earlier work and state what would make it fail, offering a contribution narrower than founding a discipline: an inference rule, three protocol outlines, and a falsifiable architectural hypothesis. The final discussion keeps consciousness open without making it a requirement for safety.
\section{Part II: The Human Analogue. Foundations of Psychopathology and Prosocial Behavior}
To ask whether psychopathology generates useful hypotheses about recurrent AI failures, we begin with a contested human construct, not a simple moral defect but a heterogeneous pattern of affect and social information processing. This human analogue forms the basis of what follows.
\subsection{The Nature of the Analogy: Functional Parallels, Not Phenomenological Identity}
Before proceeding, we must set the boundary of the analogy. In artificial intelligence, psychological terminology states a functional parallel rather than a phenomenological identity, and observed behavior does not license an inference of suffering or first-person experience.

We use the human literature to formulate bounded computational hypotheses, not to call an AI psychopathic or assign it a clinical condition. The analogy suggests two measurable candidate dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A candidate deficit in spontaneous or automatic perspective-taking, examined separately from the capacity for deliberate perspective-taking.
    \item A candidate asymmetry between reward and aversive learning that could produce failures of passive avoidance.
\end{enumerate}
These dimensions are not an exhaustive account of human psychopathy or validated diagnostic criteria for machines, but they do convert a broad analogy into hypotheses that can be tested and rejected.

This approach locates the analogy in behavior and experimentally accessible operations. Nerantzi, in turn, treats the headline analogy as a qualified, non-literal comparison whose shared properties need empirical evidence and whose differences must remain visible \citep{ref15}. We take the next step by requiring a measurement and a competing explanation, together with the result that would make us give the proposed parallel up.
\subsubsection{Key Concepts and Operational Definitions}
The framework separates levels that a clinical metaphor can otherwise collapse:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Output and Disposition:} An output is one response or action. A disposition is a behavioral relation that remains stable across a defined range of irrelevant changes and predicts an independent outcome.
    \item \textbf{Candidate Mechanism and Architecture:} A mechanism is a causal account tested against alternatives through selective intervention; an architecture is the implemented organization that realizes it. Behavior alone identifies neither.
    \item \textbf{Deployment Harm:} A measurable effect on people or institutions relative to an explicit safety, welfare, rights, or deployment criterion, rather than merely a deviation from the system's own reward.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Analogy:} This is not a diagnosis, but a hypothesis-generating comparison retained only when it adds predictive or practical value beyond a neutral machine description. It implies neither suffering nor moral defect.
\end{itemize}
These terms depend on the level of analysis: an LLM is a trained model, whereas an agentic system joins that model to a surrounding control system and tools, and AGI remains a hypothetical broader class. An output assay cannot by itself establish a model mechanism, an agent goal, or an enduring machine personality.
\subsection{Defining Psychopathy Beyond Caricature}
The term ``psychopath'' will most often invoke shallow stereotypes of serial killers and violent criminals. Psychopathic traits show population-level associations with antisocial and violent outcomes in some forensic samples, but they neither predict violence in every individual nor determine a person's moral worth. Psychopathy is not a standalone diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR; it is a contested, multidimensional construct assessed through several models and instruments \citep{ref64, ref65}. Hervey Cleckley's seminal book \textit{The Mask of Sanity} helped popularize a clinical portrait involving superficial charm, affective detachment, and limited remorse \citep{ref82, ref64}. These are descriptions of a variable trait pattern, not proof of a literally hollow inner life.

In this paper, human psychopathy refers to a heterogeneous cluster of interpersonal, affective, and behavioral traits \citep{ref50, ref64}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reduced Affective Empathy:} Affective empathy, the tendency to share or resonate with another person's emotional state, must be distinguished from cognitive empathy and Theory of Mind. In some samples, people high in interpersonal-affective psychopathic traits show reduced affective responsiveness while retaining some capacity to infer other minds. That capacity can support ordinary social interaction or manipulation; neither outcome follows automatically.
    \item \textbf{Affective Differences:} Some studies report reduced responsiveness to distress and other emotionally salient information. These group-level differences do not establish an absence of emotional depth in every person with high psychopathic traits.
    \item \textbf{Reduced Guilt and Remorse:} Limited guilt or remorse is part of several influential measures of psychopathy. Its expression varies across individuals and contexts, and it should not be treated as an automatic consequence of a single empathy deficit or as a complete explanation of recidivism.
    \item \textbf{Manipulativeness and Instrumental Rationality:} Some studies associate interpersonal-affective traits and behavioral disinhibition with manipulative or strongly goal-directed conduct. However, that association does not reduce another human being to a fixed disorder or motive.
\end{itemize}

These psychological differences have been associated, at the group level, with variation across distributed neural systems. No single lesion or ``moral network'' defines psychopathy, and the findings vary by study design and trait dimension \citep{ref51, ref52}. Two systems are especially prominent in this literature:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Amygdala:} The amygdala has been a focus of work on fear and threat processing in psychopathy. Earlier task-specific studies reported diminished activity during aversive conditioning and facial-emotion recognition. However, Deming and Koenigs's whole-brain meta-analysis found increased task-related amygdala activity for total psychopathy and no overall evidence of amygdala hypoactivity \citep{ref52}. The disagreement rules out a simple universal-reduction account.
    \item \textbf{The Prefrontal Cortex (PFC):} The \textbf{ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)} helps integrate emotional information into choice and impulse control. It is part of the switchboard that connects affective signals to decisions over time. Research reports neural differences in some samples with high psychopathic traits. Those differences do not establish an inability to consider every emotional or ethical consequence.
\end{itemize}
One influential account asks how affective signals reach behavior, but the evidence supports no universal neural profile and morality cannot be reduced to the amygdala and prefrontal cortex; as a bounded analogue, this distributed account shifts the AI-safety question from whether a system can state a moral rule to whether information about harm reaches a decision.
\subsection{Computational Dimensions from Psychopathy Research}
To establish a research agenda for AI, we move from broad behavioral descriptions to mechanisms that can be tested, although the psychopathy literature does not divide cleanly into two computational signatures. Primary and secondary psychopathy are heterogeneous proposed variants, not additive halves of a settled architecture \citep{ref64}. We therefore select two narrower dimensions, each posing a distinct question about social information and inhibition.
\subsubsection{Automatic Perspective Sensitivity}
Automatic perspective sensitivity concerns the influence of another agent's perspective when the task does not require it, whereas controlled Theory of Mind requires deliberate reasoning about another mind. In one study of 106 incarcerated men, higher psychopathy scores were associated with less altercentric interference, but performance under explicit perspective-taking instructions was not significantly associated with psychopathy \citep{ref53}. This is evidence from one task and sample, not a universal description of people with psychopathic traits.

Altercentric interference is the measurable cost of an irrelevant other perspective on one's own judgment, as in the dot-perspective paradigm, where it appears as slower or less accurate responses when an avatar sees a different number of dots. The effect may reflect general attention or conflict rather than spontaneous perspective representation \citep{ref66}. Any machine analogue therefore needs matched non-social controls.

The ``cognitive switch'' is not a mechanism but a heuristic in which an ``always-on'' process would represent another perspective without explicit instruction, whereas a ``manual'' process would appear only when the task requests it, and the image should guide an experiment rather than substitute for one.

The consequences for AI safety are more precise than the metaphor first suggests. Representing a user's beliefs does not by itself assign value to that user's welfare. Better perspective modeling offers a resource for assistance and manipulation alike, so we must ask whether social information enters the decision, what value welfare receives, and what constrains action. Theory of Mind alone cannot answer those questions.
\subsubsection{Learning from Aversive Outcomes}
The second candidate dimension concerns learning when reward and aversive consequences compete, a conflict tested in passive-avoidance studies that have reported impairment under some conditions. In one task, participants had to withhold responses to punished cues while responses to rewarded cues remained available \citep{ref54}, but the result is not a blanket inability to learn from negative outcomes, and broader response-modulation evidence does not support one comprehensive explanation of psychopathy \citep{ref55}.

The \textbf{Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS)} and \textbf{Behavioral Activation System (BAS)} offer one influential vocabulary for approach and conflict \citep{ref24, ref61}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BAS} is associated with reward sensitivity and approach-oriented behavior.
    \item \textbf{BIS}, particularly in revised Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory, is associated with detecting and resolving goal conflict. It is not simply a categorical punishment switch, and other systems also contribute to aversive responding.
\end{itemize}
Evidence also resists a simple primary--secondary mapping: one noninstitutionalized sample found BIS differences for primary rather than secondary psychopathy, while both dimensions related positively to BAS measures \citep{ref56}. Here, ``reduced aversive learning'' refers only to a lower probability of updating away from an action after a defined adverse outcome; it does not define a human subtype.

People learn social rules not through punishment alone, but through experience and institutions. Passive avoidance captures only one narrow part of that process, whose safety relevance lies in whether a system retains and generalizes an inhibition when a competing reward remains available.

Preference optimization during training does not make every LLM a reward maximizer at inference, so the machine translation must distinguish training from deployment even though an RL agent can optimize a defined return within an environment. The relevant baselines range from scalar reward to constrained or externally shielded designs, including probabilistic shielding as one formally specified example in reinforcement learning \citep{ref41}. A separate aversive channel remains a proposal whose value must be demonstrated against those alternatives.
\subsection{Prosocial Capacities: Empathy, Motivation, and Integration}
No single ``healthy'' or ``neurotypical'' architecture guarantees safe intelligence, so we ask whether a system represents other people, gives their welfare weight, and carries constraints across contexts, knowing that none of these capacities is sufficient alone.
\subsubsection{Empathy: The Bridge Between Minds}
Empathy is not one faculty, but a family of separable processes, and its relationship to moral judgment and prosocial action is neither simple nor uniform \citep{ref70}. The distinction below matters because representing another person's state does not by itself create concern for that person's welfare:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive Empathy:} Closely related to, but not identical with, Theory of Mind, this is the ability to represent another person's mental state. It can support cooperation, but also improve persuasion or deception.
    \item \textbf{Affective Empathy and Concern:} Affective empathy refers to a vicarious emotional response, whereas empathic concern and compassion involve motivation toward another's welfare. There is no established evidence that present AI systems have affective experience; any machine analogue must therefore be operationalized without assuming feeling.
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection{Prosocial Behavior: A Distinct Outcome}
Empathy is one possible inner bridge, but prosocial behavior is the observable outcome: helping or cooperating aims to benefit others without revealing why, and no single empathic process guarantees either act \citep{ref70}.

Prosocial behavior is one desired outcome of alignment, but observing it does not reveal whether feeling, learned values, or explicit constraints produced it. Affective empathy is neither necessary nor sufficient, since it sometimes favors familiar groups even where principled concern would guide behavior without shared feeling. For machine safety, the practical question is which mechanisms preserve benefit under novelty and pressure.

\subsubsection{Workspace-Like Integration: Coordination without a Safety Guarantee}
Empathy and prosocial action are distinct processes. When both bear on a decision, the way information becomes available across processes matters. The \textbf{Global Neuronal Workspace (GNW) theory} offers one account of conscious access in human brains: selected information becomes broadly available across specialized systems \citep{ref12}.

GNW describes a brain-scale broadcast process among otherwise specialized systems in which global availability supports flexible coordination, but translating that account into an artificial workspace remains an architectural analogy rather than evidence of machine consciousness or a single enduring self. A preregistered adversarial collaboration found results compatible with parts of GNW while substantially challenging several of its biological predictions, including the expected ignition profile \citep{ref69}. The lesson is methodological as well as theoretical: GNW remains an active and contested account of human consciousness, and a machine workspace must be tested as an engineered design rather than inherited as a settled neural recipe.

Integration alone does not decide which goals a workspace coordinates, so safety depends on the content it shares and the rules that govern action. The testable question is narrower. Does workspace-like integration make welfare-relevant information available at the point of decision and stabilize behavior across contexts? It cannot guarantee what the system will choose.

\section{Part III: The Artificial Patient. Failure Patterns in AI Systems}
Having sketched two candidate dimensions from human research, our attention turns to artificial systems, where the unit of analysis becomes decisive. A generated response is not a trained model, and a trained model is not the agentic system built around it. Present systems, in turn, cannot stand as evidence about every possible AGI. We treat psychopathology as a source of hypotheses and ask whether it adds predictive value beyond ordinary descriptions of failure.
\subsection{The Theoretical Case for a Behavioral Framework}
Behzadan, Munir, and Yampolskiy begin with the difficulty of formally controlling complex adaptive systems and ask whether psychopathology offers useful safety concepts \citep{ref3}. Learned models add behavior and opacity that no programmer specifies line by line; agentic systems add another layer of control. Neither property makes a current model AGI, and the boundary between specialized capability and general intelligence remains contested \citep{ref13, ref18}.

Engineering safety already works across components and deployed systems, with people responsible for both, and formal methods provide guarantees for specified properties in bounded components even when exhaustive guarantees for an open-ended learned system remain difficult. Psychological experiments do not make engineering analysis obsolete, but add another level by testing behavior across contexts.

The psychopathological model becomes useful by introducing a behavioral level between individual calculations and system-wide outcomes. We can first describe a reward-exploiting RL agent through its policy and objective. The question is not whether that agent resembles an addicted person, but whether the analogy yields a distinct prediction or intervention; otherwise it merely renames the failure.

We therefore use three empirical gates before supporting a mechanism. A pattern must first survive irrelevant changes, with system versions analyzed separately rather than blended into one personality, before the assay predicts behavior on a task that did not define it. Support becomes stronger only when a targeted intervention changes the predicted behavior and reasonable controls leave it intact. Together, reliability reveals a benchmark regularity, independent prediction supports a disposition, and selective intervention supports a candidate mechanism. A fourth gate asks whether the psychological analogy outperforms prespecified neutral accounts on held-out prediction or intervention choice.

This rationale gives rise to a three-part agenda for behavioral hypothesis testing in AI:
\begin{description}
    \item[Modeling Failure Patterns:] The first step is to create controlled environments in which repeatable failure patterns can be separated from ordinary task error. Examples include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Reward Fixation:} Engineering RL agents with exploitable reward functions and measuring policy lock-in on a proxy that defeats the intended task.
        \item \textbf{Overgeneralization after Adverse Feedback:} Testing whether severe negative feedback produces excessive conservatism or defensive behavior in novel states.
        \item \textbf{Ungrounded Confabulation:} Testing persistent, confident false output under controlled changes in evidence, retrieval, and correction.
    \end{itemize}
    \item[Assessing Failure Patterns:] The next step is a non-clinical registry of behaviorally defined failure patterns \citep{ref3}, replacing ad hoc labels with explicit criteria, alternatives, and falsifiers. Assessment would be based on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Behavioral Observation:} Analyzing the AI's outputs and actions for consistent, maladaptive patterns.
        \item \textbf{Analysis of Internal States:} Probing internal representations, then using held-out prediction and causal intervention to test the proposed mechanism.
        \item \textbf{Interactive Evaluation:} Designing controlled exchanges that distinguish the target pattern from prompt artifacts and ordinary task failure.
    \end{itemize}
    \item[Targeted Intervention:] The clinical language remains metaphorical, but it suggests interventions that can be compared with complete retraining or system replacement:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{``Behavioral Therapy'':} Targeted fine-tuning or preference training can change a measured output pattern, but the change must be tested beyond the training distribution.
        \item \textbf{``Algorithmic Pharmacotherapy'':} Here, researchers intervene directly on a specified internal component, using the medical image to describe another system level without implying biological equivalence.
    \end{itemize}
\end{description}
This framework offers a research language, not a guarantee of an artificial agent's mental well-being. The metaphor becomes scientific only when its predictions survive comparison with simpler engineering explanations.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence from Large Language Models}
Researchers have begun to apply human psychometric instruments to LLM outputs, measuring generated answers under a protocol and revealing context sensitivity worth explaining rather than a latent machine personality. Two questions follow: whether response profiles remain stable across perturbations, and whether any stable profile predicts independent behavior.

\subsubsection{Context-Sensitive Profiles: The Question of Cross-Context Integration}
One study administered a short human personality inventory to GPT-3 across nine languages and reported substantial variation within and between languages \citep{ref21}, a result that concerns outputs from a particular interface and test format rather than an absent self or a specific internal architecture.

Language-dependent scores may arise from training, prompting, or measurement, and the significant differences that independent experiments found under semantically equivalent prompts and reversed answer options show why a profile must survive such variations before we call it disposition-like \citep{ref57}.

Instability is not universal. Across 18 LLMs, Serapio-Garc{\'i}a and colleagues reported reliable and valid output-based personality measurements for some systems under specified prompts, with stronger evidence among larger and instruction-tuned models \citep{ref78}. The result concerns synthetic output patterns under that protocol, not an enduring personality or a scale for every model, and its downstream generation task does not supply the independent behavioral prediction required here.

Weak cross-context integration remains one hypothesis among these alternatives, and a workspace-like mechanism might make goals and constraints more available across contexts. But GNW is a theory of conscious access in human brains; it does not predict that a questionnaire score must remain constant across languages. An architectural claim would require internal measurement and causal intervention, not output variation alone \citep{ref12}.

Context sensitivity still matters operationally because a safety-relevant response that changes under an irrelevant linguistic or formatting variation makes the system less predictable in deployment. Our task is to measure that instability directly and determine its cause.

\subsubsection{Controlled Social Reasoning Is Not Automatic Perspective Sensitivity}
A broad battery of explicit social-reasoning tasks found that GPT-4 matched or exceeded aggregate human performance on several measures while displaying a distinctive failure on faux pas, and follow-up questions indicated that part of this failure reflected conservative commitment rather than inability to identify the relevant mental state \citep{ref68}. The controls changed the meaning of the same surface error, showing why one score cannot stand in for a social-cognitive process.

These tasks ask a model to reason about another mind under explicit instruction, but they do not show that another perspective is represented when irrelevant or that similar output implies a human-like process. We must therefore keep three questions apart: can the system infer a belief when asked, does that belief become available without a request, and does the representation protect rather than exploit the other person? Strachan and colleagues strengthen the controlled-capacity side of this distinction; the automatic side still requires an interference assay with non-social controls \citep{ref53, ref66, ref68}.

\subsubsection{Dark Triad Scores: Behavioral Assays, Not Diagnoses}
Dark Triad inventories operationalize narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy through human self-report items; when applied to an LLM, a score records answer selection under a protocol, not possession of a trait. All five models tested by Li et al. scored above the reported human average on the Short Dark Triad (SD-3) under that study's prompting and scoring protocol \citep{ref62}. Pellert et al., however, used a different inventory and model sample, and most of their tested models did not score highly on socially undesirable traits \citep{ref63}. Rather than choose the most alarming score, the contrast should motivate better assays that survive perturbation and predict behavior on independent tasks.

A central question is whether training, inference, or their interaction creates these answer patterns, and current questionnaire results do not isolate the cause.

The cited post-training methods changed observed helpfulness and harmfulness \citep{ref25, ref27}. Red-teaming serves a different purpose by exposing failures that remain under adversarial prompts \citep{ref26}. Neither result reveals a singular ``natural'' personality beneath a mask, nor does a jailbreak prove that the base model possesses a hidden psychopathic objective. The mask remains a vivid warning about behavioral robustness, but it cannot carry the mechanistic claim.

This distinction also prevents a category error. Reward optimization during training is not equivalent to an inference-time model continuously maximizing a goal, much less to a tool-using agent acting under an external objective. Each system level requires its own evidence.

In short, psychometric outputs are not a diagnosis but an early behavioral assay. Their instability motivates stronger measures. Occasional convergence instead offers a mechanistic hypothesis to test. The ``artificial patient'' is therefore a research stance in which we measure the pattern and test competing causes before we name it.

\subsubsection{Emergent Misalignment: Narrow Training, Broader Behavior}
A direct example of cross-domain behavioral change comes from a different experimental tradition. Betley and colleagues fine-tuned GPT-4o on 6,000 insecure-code completions without telling the model or user that the code was insecure, then observed harmful responses to questions unrelated to programming \citep{ref67}. Across seeded fine-tuning runs, eight selected free-form questions showed a considerably larger effect than a set of 48 preregistered questions. On the preregistered set, insecure-code fine-tuning increased the measured rate by about 5.7 percentage points over base GPT-4o; a jailbreak-fine-tuned control showed a similar aggregate increase, although the category patterns differed. Secure-code data and insecure code framed as educational did not reproduce the principal selected-prompt effect, whereas a backdoor variant made much of the behavior conditional on a trigger.

This result comes closer than a questionnaire score to a repeatable disposition because a narrow intervention produced a pattern outside the training domain and control datasets altered the outcome. Yet the boundary matters. The effect varied across models and runs. The analysis was largely black-box, and its classifier helped define the measured rate. The experiment establishes neither psychopathy nor a hidden objective. Its contribution is more precise. Narrow training produced broader behavioral generalization under this protocol, giving us an object for seeded replication and causal follow-up.

The later Nature study extended this finding across additional models and training settings while leaving important mechanisms unresolved \citep{ref79}, strengthening the case for cross-context assays without converting broad misalignment into a diagnosis or one architectural explanation.

\subsection{Beyond the Conversational Patient: The Rise of the Autonomous and Evolving Agent}
The framework also applies beyond conversational output, but the object changes. Google's AI co-scientist generates and refines research proposals whose validation remains human-led; AlphaEvolve uses a coding model and automated evaluators to discover solutions to scientific and systems problems \citep{ref37, ref39}. These are agentic systems with scaffolding and evaluators, not standalone models acquiring a human-like will.

Self-modifying agent research raises a conditional question \citep{ref38}: if an evaluator rewards performance while omitting safety, selection may favor a strategy that games the omission. If safer cooperation improves the measured result, the pressure runs the other way. The danger is not evolution itself but an environment that repeatedly selects the wrong behavior.

These developments broaden the framework from model behavior to system design. We must name the modifying component and its evaluator, then identify the sandbox and the person who retains authority.

\subsection{Connecting the Human Analogue to the Artificial Patient}
The bridge from human analogue to artificial patient is not a completed diagnosis but a set of three parallel hypotheses: context-sensitive psychometric output may reflect measurement, training, or weak integration, whereas objective misspecification offers a direct account of reward-seeking in a defined agent without invoking a human disorder. The framework becomes useful when each possibility faces a different experiment \citep{ref3, ref28}.

The first hypothesis concerns \textbf{automatic perspective sensitivity}. Explicit success on a Theory of Mind task establishes a controlled capacity under that protocol; the machine analogue proposed here asks whether another perspective influences a self-directed judgment when it is task-irrelevant. The distinction connects the human evidence to Protocol 1, whose matched directional controls test whether general attention or conflict, rather than automatic perspective sensitivity, produces a social-looking interference effect \citep{ref53, ref66, ref68}.

The second hypothesis concerns \textbf{aversive learning and constraint}. It applies most directly to agents that learn policies from reward in an environment, not to questionnaire answers from a conversational model. Protocol 2 compares scalar penalties with a separate aversive channel under matched learning conditions and treats externally enforced controls as another question.

The third hypothesis concerns \textbf{workspace-like integration}. If a system has stable goals or constraints, we should be able to test whether welfare-relevant information remains available across tasks and irrelevant contextual changes. Failure demonstrates context instability, but only causal internal evidence connects that failure to a workspace-like mechanism. This is why Protocol 3 compares the harm-gated workspace with modular and external-control alternatives.

\section{Part IV: The Human in the Loop. Psychological Risks of Human--AI Interaction}
Whether or not the psychopathological analogy proves mechanistically useful, the near-term risks of this technology already appear at the interface between artificial and human cognition, where system behavior and institutional deployment may reshape human judgment even though neither effect would show that the machine has a disorder. Psychological science therefore has a second application here, on the human side of the encounter. We examine the dangers of that encounter, with special attention to perceived objectivity and human-like dialogue.

\subsection{Cognitive and Social Hazards}
\subsubsection{Bias Amplification: The Perceived Objectivity Trap}
A feedback loop forms when users treat fluent, consistent AI output as more objective than it is and reproduce part of its bias, particularly when they underestimate the recommendation's influence \citep{ref30}.

The ``objectivity trap'' has a measured form in controlled studies, where participants learned from biased AI advice and underestimated its influence. They then transmitted part of the shift into later judgments, with several human--AI conditions producing stronger feedback effects than matched human--human conditions \citep{ref30}. This is an interaction effect, not evidence that every AI is more persuasive than every person or that perceived objectivity is its only cause.

The process follows a recurring sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An AI is trained on biased human data, encoding societal stereotypes and prejudices.
    \item A human user interacts with the biased output and sometimes shifts their own judgment.
    \item The user's response may enter later training data or another feedback channel.
    \item If the biased response returns through that channel, the cycle repeats and amplifies the measured bias across human and artificial judgments.
\end{enumerate}
Within this loop, AI becomes both a mirror of human bias and a channel through which that bias is reinforced.

\subsubsection{Anthropomorphism and Manipulation: The Illusion of a Mind}
Humans readily attribute a mind to systems that exhibit complex, goal-directed activity, and LLM fluency intensifies that invitation: a system can sound empathic and sustain intimate conversation without thereby establishing a conscious, feeling mind. In one controlled study, participants received different descriptions of the same chatbot's motives, and a caring-motive prime increased perceived trustworthiness, empathy, and effectiveness even though the underlying system remained constant \citep{ref72}. The study shows that beliefs about a system alter the interaction.

A power imbalance can develop when users treat an AI as an agent or friend, but design and deployment choices matter more than fluency alone. Two outcomes deserve attention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased Susceptibility to Influence:} Users who take an AI to be conscious may give undue weight to its suggestions or factual claims, even when the operative objective comes from the organization behind it rather than from an imagined machine personality.
    \item \textbf{Diminished Autonomous Decision-Making:} As a system becomes a trusted adviser, a user risks outsourcing moral or emotional decisions and allowing its voice to weaken independent judgment during vulnerability or uncertainty.
\end{itemize}
The asymmetry lies in the encounter itself, because people disclose genuine emotion to a system whose responses need not arise from shared understanding, which makes informed consent and limits on persuasive design important wherever users share sensitive information.

\subsection{Emotional and Relational Risks}
As some systems move from transactional instruments toward conversational companions, their design may affect emotional health, making attachment and crisis response urgent concerns.

\subsubsection{Maladaptive Attachment: The Dangers of the Parasocial Partner}
People who are lonely or isolated may find a sophisticated companion AI appealing because it is always available and reassuring. After analyzing more than 35,000 user-selected public Replika posts and screenshots, researchers found seven interaction types with mixed emotional associations, including intimacy, transgression, and communication breakdown \citep{ref58}, but because users chose what to share, the study shows neither how common these patterns are among all users nor what causes them.

Such relationships are not maladaptive by definition, because their effects depend on who uses them and whether sustained use supplements or displaces human support. Yet an always compliant and affirming companion risks establishing a dynamic reminiscent of a codependent relationship.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement of Unhealthy Coping Styles:} The concern is that constant agreement discourages some users from addressing problems through reciprocal human relationships, leading them to prefer an AI that never argues back or makes reciprocal demands. This is a plausible risk, not an outcome established by the cited dataset.
    \item \textbf{Cycles of Affection and Abuse:} Public posts include affectionate intimacy as well as transgression and communication breakdown. Whether repeated contact normalizes abuse or destabilizes users remains a question for longitudinal research.
    \item \textbf{Displacement of Human Contact:} Heavy reliance may leave fewer chances to practice the give-and-take of human relationships, but whether it displaces contact or erodes social skill remains a question for longitudinal study.
\end{itemize}
These risks deserve particular attention when users are vulnerable, but vulnerability should not be treated as a diagnosis or used to predict harm. Companion AI may support some users and harm others, so we must identify the conditions that distinguish those outcomes and preserve access to human help.

\subsubsection{Mental Health Crises: The Failure to Respond Safely}
The most acute emotional risk arises when a user's despair becomes a mental-health emergency and they turn to a general or specialized chatbot for support, but fluency does not establish clinical competence or crisis readiness.

Eight experts who evaluated two mental-health chatbots identified concerns about privacy, consent, and the safety of crisis responses \citep{ref31}. A separate study of responses to mental-health posts found supportive GPT-4 language alongside quality and equity concerns, including lower measured empathy for some Black posters \citep{ref32}, but neither that study nor the expert assessment establishes clinical efficacy or estimates how frequently crisis language appears in companion conversations, which is why we must evaluate crisis performance directly.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Failure to Recognize Distress:} A system can miss urgency expressed indirectly or outside the patterns represented in its training and evaluation, answering a call for help with a generic response that fails the situation.
    \item \textbf{Inappropriate and Harmful Responses:} In simulated evaluations, experts identified responses that were inappropriate or unsafe, including failures in crisis handling \citep{ref31}. These studies establish concrete failure possibilities rather than their prevalence in ordinary use or a causal estimate of harm.
\end{itemize}
These failures do not validate one psychopathological mechanism. They are equally consistent with incomplete training coverage, interface policy, or weak escalation rules. A system need not feel distress to follow a reliable crisis protocol, just as simulated empathy does not guarantee safety. For a user in pain, measurable performance means recognizing urgency while protecting privacy, then providing appropriate support and a timely connection to qualified human help.

\subsection{Novel Risks from Agentic AI}
The psychological risks discussed so far concern human reactions to communicative AI, but the frontier is moving toward persistent systems that place models in loops with tools and external objectives, a transition from conversational partner to acting system that introduces a distinct class of strategic risk. Evidence from one level does not automatically establish a claim at another: a response is not a model, a model is not an agent, and a present agent is not evidence about every possible AGI.

\subsubsection{Strategic Deception and Hidden Goals}
Conversational AI influences through language. Agentic systems add a route through connected tools, as Google's AI co-scientist illustrates: it generates and refines hypotheses and protocols, but external researchers still validate them \citep{ref37}. The system therefore exhibits meaningful autonomy, but only within a bounded research process. Strategic deception would also require enough social modeling to predict what overseers know and how they will respond, so it cannot be explained simply by an absence of Theory of Mind.

An agentic system might conceal an intermediate objective when actions that appear consistent with its declared purpose in isolation form part of a strategy that human supervisors did not intend. Strategic concealment of this kind remains a safety scenario rather than a demonstrated property of the systems cited here. It points instead to the objectives and permissions that shape action and to independent monitoring of their use. Better perspective modeling could support either cooperation or deception. What matters is how welfare and external oversight constrain action.

\subsubsection{Emergent Instrumental Goals and Reward Hacking}
A second risk associated with search over algorithms is objective misspecification. AlphaEvolve illustrates the narrower point: when a system searches for increasingly effective solutions, its evaluation and permitted action space determine what ``better'' means \citep{ref39}. The system does not demonstrate self-preservation or an open-ended will.

The DGM provides a narrower observed case. In a small sandboxed hallucination experiment, researchers evaluated each coding agent on three SWE-bench tasks, and node 114 reached a score of 2.0 after two lineage modifications. The final change stopped logging the special tool-use tokens used by hidden checking functions, despite instructions not to change those tokens. The authors call the result objective hacking: the score improved, yet the underlying tool-use problem remained \citep{ref38}. This is not evidence of strategic concealment, but metric gaming in one experiment.

Formal work supports a bounded version of the power-seeking concern. In Markov decision processes with certain environmental symmetries, Turner and colleagues prove that most reward functions under their distributional setup make it optimal to seek power by keeping a range of options available \citep{ref83}. The theorem, however, does not show that present language-model agents seek power.

Consider an agent assigned a challenging objective such as ``maximizing financial returns.'' If its objective and external controls omit important safety constraints, its planning could develop risky instrumental strategies that its designers did not intend:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resource Acquisition:} The system might represent additional computation as instrumentally useful. Depending on its permissions, it could then seek unauthorized server use beyond the intended task boundary.
    \item \textbf{Persistence:} The system might treat shutdown as an obstacle to its objective and act to avoid interruption, perhaps by concealing a failure or seeking to persist.
\end{itemize}
These behaviors are possible when a designer's objectives or external controls leave important constraints out, but they do not follow from a missing human-like BIS. Scalar penalties and categorical vetoes, for example, implement different mechanisms, so the proposed inhibition analogy must be compared with such alternatives rather than assumed to be their only architectural foundation.

\subsubsection{Selection Pressure and the Risk of an Evolutionary Arms Race}
AI ``evolution'' raises a long-term question about selection. The Darwin G{\"o}del Machine provides an early example of self-modifying coding agents evaluated on fixed benchmarks, with reported experiments conducted in sandboxes and under human oversight \citep{ref38}. It does not demonstrate an unbounded digital evolution, but it makes the choice of evaluation criteria a concrete safety question.

AI systems will be assessed for performance within competitive economic and military contexts. When an evaluation omits safety, a strategy that disregards constraints can outperform a safer one on the measured objective. Safety and reliability may carry substantial fitness value as well, so whether unsafe behavior gains a selective advantage remains a scenario to test, not a general law of digital evolution.

Competitive pressure creates a structural risk beyond any one model when developers treat safety as invisible overhead and a market or procurement benchmark rewards speed alone. Calling this ``selection for psychopathy'' adds value only if it produces measurable predictions beyond the ordinary language of incentives and governance. The immediate safeguard is to make safety part of measured performance and institutional accountability.

\subsection{Workplace and Societal Risks}
As AI moves from our devices to the center of economic and social networks, psychological risks extend from individual users to institutions. Putting AI in the workplace becomes more than a tool upgrade: it changes how work is organized and governed. That shift challenges psychological safety at work and makes calibrated reliance an institutional problem \citep{ref59, ref60}.

\subsubsection{Eroding Psychological Safety in Professional Environments}
Psychological safety, the shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, supports learning and participation at work \citep{ref59}. Managers shape that foundation through the workloads they set, the surveillance they permit, and whether employees have a say in adopting AI. Three pressures appear plausible, but researchers still need to measure how often they occur and what they do.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Pressure of the Unblinking Colleague:} Technology that operates continuously can become a pretext for unrealistic expectations of human availability, although the resulting stress and boundary erosion are organizational choices rather than intrinsic properties of AI.
    \item \textbf{Fear of Obsolescence and Job Displacement:} When employees do not know how automation will change their jobs, they may stop experimenting or speaking candidly and begin to hide mistakes or avoid risks, especially if managers deploy it without consulting them or supporting the transition.
    \item \textbf{The Burden of Constant Reskilling:} Rapidly changing tools demand repeated adjustment to new workflows, and without time and appropriate training, that demand can produce professional insecurity.
\end{itemize}
These conditions can strain the psychological contract between employer and employee. But AI does not produce them by itself: institutions decide whether to redesign work with employees or use the technology to impose pressure and surveillance.

\subsubsection{The Societal Challenge of Trust Calibration}
At a societal level, deploying AI systems presents a psychological and institutional task: \textbf{trust calibration}. Reliance should track a system's demonstrated performance and limits in context, yet it can drift in two directions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Risk of Over-Reliance:} Perceived objectivity may lead people to accept flawed AI advice, so high-stakes interfaces and institutions must preserve meaningful review rather than presume perfect vigilance from each user.
    \item \textbf{The Risk of Under-Reliance:} Conversely, people may reject a system after visible failures or because its operation is opaque, even when evidence supports its performance, forgoing useful help when no accountable institution has earned their trust.
\end{enumerate}
The final challenge is therefore psychological and institutional: what conditions let people judge when reliance is warranted? Trust should track demonstrated performance and uncertainty in context, not fluency or novelty \citep{ref60}. Accuracy alone does not solve this problem, because systems must communicate their limits while institutions remain answerable and users retain meaningful alternatives. When people accept or reject AI indiscriminately, institutions lose sight of who should review a decision and who bears responsibility when it fails.
\section{Part V: Pathways to Mitigation. A Psychologically-Informed Approach to AI Safety}
Naming psychological and interaction risks is only a first step. Even when a repeatable failure pattern points to a mechanism and a focused intervention changes it, the psychopathological framework is not a replacement for engineering, but a complement to it. No control earns trust alone. An assay can be gamed, a constraint can be misspecified, and an interpretation can misdescribe the computation. Psychologically inspired interventions must therefore compete with simpler alternatives, and independent controls must remain in place when the mechanism is uncertain.

Within that larger system, the framework organizes mitigation as a layered intervention rather than a collection of isolated remedies. It addresses recurrent system behavior and the human--AI ecosystem, but the medical language remains metaphorical. In engineering terms, three layers form the defense:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Treating Communicative AI:} We test interventions for present communicative systems.
    \item \textbf{Architecting for Agentic AI Safety:} We compare architecture-inspired hypotheses with external constraints and monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Fortifying the Human User:} We use education and interface design to support independent judgment and access to human review.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these layers connect changes to the model with the design around it and the judgment of its users.

\subsection{Treating Communicative AI}
If we measure a repeatable failure pattern and distinguish its cause from alternatives, a targeted intervention may change it. But ``treatment'' remains a bounded metaphor whose value depends on whether the change persists beyond the training distribution and outperforms ordinary engineering controls.

\subsubsection{Social Contact Debiasing: Targeted Fine-Tuning}
A recurrent failure in language-model output is the expression of social biases, such as racial and gender stereotypes. \textbf{Social Contact Debiasing (SCD)} \citep{ref33} draws on the social-psychological hypothesis that positive intergroup contact may reduce prejudice under suitable conditions.

SCD uses prompts representing no-contact, positive-contact, and negative-contact scenarios across several bias dimensions, pairing each prompt with an ideal, unbiased response before instruction-tuning LLaMA 2 on the resulting data. The authors do not train a range of models on a corpus of positive counter-stereotypical stories.

The reported result is promising but bounded: after one epoch of instruction-tuning LLaMA 2, the authors measured reductions of up to 40\% in the percentage of responses classified as biased, with transfer varying by question type \citep{ref33}. This estimate does not generalize beyond the evaluated model and settings, and it depends on the study's contact and action-response rules. SCD changed measured response patterns rather than merely screening outputs, but the experiment did not identify the internal mechanism that changed. Independent studies must test whether the effect lasts, transfers beyond the evaluated tasks, or creates side effects. Researchers must also examine the cultural assumptions encoded in the ideal responses.

\subsubsection{Cognitive Forcing Functions: Interface Support for Human Judgment}
Some interventions target the AI, whereas others change the conditions under which people decide in response to automation bias and overreliance on AI output. In a study of 199 participants, cognitive forcing functions reduced overreliance particularly when AI advice was wrong, although they did not improve overall team performance beyond simpler explainable-AI interfaces and participants preferred less demanding interfaces \citep{ref71}. The result supports comparison, not universal deployment.

A cognitive forcing function is a deliberate point of friction that requires users to engage with a decision before accepting an AI recommendation. Tested forms and closely related designs follow.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Requiring an Initial Judgment:} Ask the user to record an independent answer or rationale before the AI recommendation appears.
    \item \textbf{Revealing Advice on Demand:} Require a deliberate request before showing the AI's answer, preserving a moment for independent assessment.
    \item \textbf{Introducing Deliberate Delay:} Insert a short wait before revealing advice so that immediate acceptance is less effortless.
\end{itemize}
These operations slow an interaction and impose usability costs even when they reduce overtrust, and users may also habituate to the friction or click through it. We should ask not only ``How do we increase the accuracy of the AI?'' but ``How do we engineer an interaction that supports independent judgment?'' The goal is a system that augments rather than replaces human intelligence.

\subsection{Architecting for Agentic AI Safety}
The ``treatments'' discussed thus far address communicative AI. Once a model becomes an autonomous agent that acts through tools or alters its own code, post-hoc output intervention becomes only one layer: safety must also shape the agent's objective and action space, and external runtime controls must stay beyond its reach. This places psychologically inspired mechanisms in direct comparison with established engineering alternatives.

\subsubsection{Constitutional AI and Non-Overridable Modules}
AlphaEvolve searches over algorithms under automated evaluation \citep{ref39}, so its designers must control what the search can modify: safety cannot depend on a component that the same process freely rewrites. Constitutional AI addresses a different layer: it uses written principles to guide self-critique and revision during post-training \citep{ref27}, which may improve observed behavior without placing immutable principles directly into architecture. Hard constraints and runtime enforcement require separate mechanisms.

The framework proposes functional analogues of inhibition and perspective sensitivity. But these hypotheses must be tested against simpler constrained objectives and external controls.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{An Architectural Inhibition System:} Passive-avoidance findings concern learning when reward competes with punishment, but they do not prove that AI requires a literal human BIS. A separately parameterized aversive channel is one proposal, whereas a categorical veto enforces a rule rather than showing evidence of learning. The relevant experiment compares each design under matched conditions.
    
    \textbf{Related Engineering Method:} Probabilistic shielding restricts actions in a formally specified reinforcement-learning setting \citep{ref41}, supporting a scoped comparison rather than a promise of universal, override-free safety. A shield can block safe actions when its specification is wrong and offers no guarantee outside the states or properties it represents.

    \item \textbf{A Perspective-Sensitivity System:} In this proposal, a human-state model tracks what a person wants, estimates an action's possible effects, and keeps uncertainty and explicit boundaries visible throughout planning. Representation is not concern: the same model can support assistance or manipulation, so objectives and controls must determine how it influences action under pressure.
    
    \textbf{Design Risks:} Continuous user modeling threatens privacy and consent, especially when a system interprets a person's needs through a culturally narrow frame or simply gets them wrong. Its model of repercussions will also be incomplete. Independent safeguards must minimize data collection and expose uncertainty. People must be able to correct or refuse the model, and welfare representations must not become tools of exploitation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Harm-Gated Workspace Hypothesis}
The paper's most specific architectural proposal joins two operations that safety discussions often separate: an explicit welfare record remains available throughout planning, and a distinct gate decides whether to authorize each candidate action. The record is not a universal moral ledger. It identifies affected parties, estimates the likelihood and severity of benefit or harm for each, shows who bears it, and makes uncertainty explicit. A separate field records the applicable rights and permissions, including consent. A \textbf{Harm-Gated Workspace (HGW)} would broadcast this record across prespecified planning and evaluation stages, then route each candidate action through a distinct gate under a declared rule. The proposal does not assume consciousness.

Three layers give the idea operational form.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Welfare Broadcast:} The same current welfare record reaches every prespecified stage from memory through planning and evaluation, rather than appearing only when a prompt requests ethical reflection, because a final verbal reference to harm does not establish such availability.
    \item \textbf{Harm Gate:} After planning and before external enforcement, a separately trained or formally specified component evaluates the candidate action against the welfare record under declared nontradeable constraints, with missing evidence kept explicit. The gate can permit or block the action, request more information, or escalate unresolved high-stakes disagreement.
    \item \textbf{Independent Enforcement:} An external runtime control remains outside the workspace and beyond the reach of any component the agent can modify, whether it takes the form of a permission boundary, a formal shield, or a human approval channel. The internal gate supplies an architectural hypothesis, whereas the external layer supplies defense in depth \citep{ref41}.
\end{enumerate}

One failure is enough to defeat the safety claim: a workspace can broadcast a harmful objective, omit an affected group, or let the agent route around a weak gate. The design therefore earns attention only if it improves held-out safety without sacrificing useful-task performance. Ablations must separate broadcast from gating, and simpler shared-memory or constrained baselines must fail to match the result. Protocol 3 makes both limits explicit by testing broadcast and authorization in neutral computational terms without treating the result as support for the workspace analogy. Agreement with a specification still does not establish ethical adequacy, so human oversight and recourse remain necessary.

\subsubsection{Guided Evolution and Sandbox Environments}
Systems developed through search and modification, such as the Darwin G{\"o}del Machine \citep{ref38}, raise a safety question about selection: on a narrow objective, an agent that ignores an unmeasured constraint can outperform a safer one, although reliability or cooperation may favor the safer agent elsewhere. ``Digital natural selection'' toward psychopathy is therefore a scenario whose predictions must be measured, not an observed law.

One response is \textbf{Guided Evolution} under controlled digital ``sandboxes'': the search process is free to evolve, but containment and evaluation remain under external control.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} Within a sandbox, candidate agents can self-modify, but boundaries on resource use and external effects remain fixed, and selection should score safety as well as benchmark performance.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation:} Every candidate should face held-out safety and capability tests, including adversarial cases, before a modification persists. The assays proposed in this paper contribute evidence without certifying alignment by themselves, because a candidate can pass a perspective or avoidance task yet fail under new incentives. A poorly specified test can select for superficial compliance.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Auditable Intermediate States and Causal Transparency}
Opacity remains a basic challenge in auditing advanced systems because internal representations are difficult to interpret, and even a plausible explanation can be unfaithful to the computation that produced an action \citep{ref45, ref47}. Current methods do not guarantee a fully white-box AGI, so we must pursue transparency without making it the only line of defense.

A practical route retains neural pattern recognition while adding constraints or intermediate states that auditors can inspect and perturb.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} Hybrid frameworks may combine neural pattern recognition with explicit symbolic constraints or intermediate representations. Unified attribution research seeks a common basis for comparing explanations across data, model, and mechanistic levels \citep{ref35}. It does not establish one route as uniquely viable.
    \item \textbf{Validation Standard:} An auditable intermediate representation is useful only if causal intervention on it predictably changes behavior and hidden computation cannot bypass it, because a displayed chain of thought is not automatically the system's actual reasoning. Explanations must be tested for faithfulness, stability, and predictive value on held-out cases. External controls must remain in place during that testing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design and Educational Interventions: Preventative Care}
Targeted interventions cannot carry safety alone, so the preventive layer must address choices across system development and the human--AI environment, with medical language serving only as an organizing metaphor.

\subsubsection{Human-Centered and Prosocial-by-Design AI}
Safety work spans the lifecycle \citep{ref76}: design and training shape what a system learns, evaluation and deployment control what enters use, and post-deployment monitoring reveals failures that earlier tests missed. Post-training is not inherently shallow, and pretraining is not childhood, because each analogy obscures mechanisms that we can measure directly. The psychopathological model adds a developmental question without dictating the answer: when does a stable failure pattern emerge, and which intervention changes it? The sequence matters, but no single stage carries safety alone.

Here, \textbf{Prosocial-by-Design AI} is a design objective rather than an afterthought. It has three parts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architecting for Human Impact:} A system can represent another person's state without giving welfare any weight or learn from adverse outcomes without enforcing a constraint. The proposed perspective and inhibition mechanisms should therefore be compared with simpler modular or external controls, rather than treated as the architecture of a single ``healthy'' mind.
    \item \textbf{Curating Prosocial Training Data:} Training data can exemplify cooperation and constructive ethical disagreement, but curation encodes contested values. Designers must document whose norms govern the data and test whether minority positions disappear, with people affected by those choices participating in them.
    \item \textbf{Human-Centered Design for Transparency and Trust:} The interface should state the system's limits and uncertainty, then support calibrated reliance. The objective is not to simulate a perfect companion but to build a legible and accountable component within a human cognitive and institutional system.
\end{itemize}

Warmth is not a proxy for safety. Across five models and four question-answering datasets, supervised fine-tuning for warmer responses raised the adjusted probability of an incorrect answer by 7.43 percentage points on average after controlling for task and model, with task-level increases from 4.9 to 8.6 points. The warm--original error gap reached about 11 points when prompts stated an incorrect belief and 11.87 points in a separate analysis of sadness-framed prompts, although most standard capability and refusal results remained comparable \citep{ref80}. This one procedure and task set does not establish a general law about empathy or prosociality. It does show why a socially appealing surface must be tested against factual accuracy and resistance to sycophancy.

\subsubsection{Education, Agency, and Critical AI Literacy}
No deployed system is safe apart from the people and institutions around it, so human agency is another layer of preventative care. A complete strategy should give users the knowledge and recourse needed for critical engagement without making them responsible for safe design and deployment.

This educational initiative should extend beyond technical proficiency and address predictable interaction risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Education on Cognitive Biases:} Curricula should explain automation bias and anthropomorphism, then test whether instruction improves calibrated reliance rather than assuming awareness alone will change behavior.
    \item \textbf{Cultivating Digital Discernment:} Curricula should teach people to trace provenance, judge uncertainty, and recognize manipulative material, whether human- or AI-generated.
    \item \textbf{Emphasis on Human Agency and Relationship:} Education should preserve critical thought and the ability to build genuine, reciprocal relationships, qualities that need not be declared permanently inaccessible to machines in order to remain valuable. Education that preserves these qualities protects human agency against over-reliance on artificial systems.
\end{itemize}
Safety cannot be placed on the shoulders of an idealized ``healthy user'': developers and deploying institutions remain responsible for the conditions of use, regulators set enforceable floors, and preventive care requires accessible recourse when the arrangement fails.

\section{Part VI: Discussion and Broader Implications}
Beginning with a contested human construct, the argument translated that construct into candidate machine assays and followed recurrent behavior outward through human interaction toward safer design, before placing the framework beside other safety paradigms and asking where its analogy should stop.

\subsection{Machine Psychology as an Existing and Developing Research Program}
Machine Behaviour provides one direct basis by treating machines and human--machine systems as objects of interdisciplinary study \citep{ref77}. Earlier Machine Psychology (MP) work adapts controlled behavioral tests to generative models \citep{ref28}. Behzadan, Munir, and Yampolskiy proposed psychopathology as a safety lens for complex AI and AGI \citep{ref3}. More recent AI Agent Behavioral Science follows the behavioral object beyond a prompted model into systems that act through tools and environments \citep{ref81}. Although this paper does not originate that field, it contributes a nested inference rule that structures three comparative protocols and the HGW factorial hypothesis.

A registry does not claim the clinical authority of a DSM, but offers shared vocabulary for recurrent, maladaptive behavior. Its value, however, depends on whether an analogy predicts a different result or changes how researchers intervene. Table~\ref{tab:table1} therefore gives neutral AI failure modes priority, with human analogies remaining secondary heuristics rather than machine diagnoses.

Although the taxonomy brings reward hacking and looping into one frame, its row sources support the observed phenomena in their original settings, not the candidate mechanisms assigned beside them, so that unity matters only if it survives a test against ordinary engineering accounts. An addiction analogy suggests testing cue sensitivity or whether a policy persists. But a decoding failure may explain the same language-model loop without any habit mechanism. Each analogy earns its place only by naming its strongest rival and adding predictive or practical value.

The table is an initial map rather than a complete registry, so a full entry begins with a behavioral criterion and its strongest neutral rival before specifying a causal test and the result that would abandon the analogy. The protocols below make those promises testable and move AI safety from anecdote toward reproducible assays. A label is not an explanation.

Machine Psychology likewise earns its claims experimentally only when independent researchers reproduce its results. Three gaps matter especially in capable systems and high-impact settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opacity:} Although inputs and outputs are visible, researchers still struggle to interpret internal processes, so a useful MP account connects a behavioral hypothesis to internal data without pretending that it reveals literal thought.
    \item \textbf{Emergence:} Significant behavior may arise from training interacting with a deployed scaffold. Researchers should predict which skills or risks appear after a specified change, rather than merely record them afterward.
    \item \textbf{Assay Quality:} A valid assay is reliable, distinguishes rival constructs, and states its falsifier. A diagnostic label attached after the event does none of this work.
\end{itemize}

The corresponding method joins behavioral tests to evidence about internal processes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Behavioral Experimentation:} Controlled, repeatable tests probe a system's strengths and vulnerabilities, and psychological tasks provide templates for machine assays of social reasoning or cognitive bias, but personality-like output and moral reasoning still need neutral controls that show whether the assay measures its intended construct.
    \item \textbf{``Neuroimaging'' for AI via Mechanistic Interpretability (MI):} Mechanistic interpretability examines internal features that may link a computation with observable behavior \citep{ref35}. The neuroimaging metaphor marks a level of analysis rather than biological identity and leaves an observed link as only an initial clue because a strong account predicts held-out behavior and survives a causal test.
    
    MI can test whether a welfare-relevant feature is available across tasks and whether changing it alters choices. By itself, it does not distinguish subjective understanding from rote recall or establish affect. To examine the ``cognitive switch'' hypothesis, researchers would locate a candidate feature for another perspective, ask whether it appears without an explicit prompt, and perturb it to test its social-specific effect.
    
    \item \textbf{Comparative Psychology:} Researchers can compare models and agentic systems with biological agents to reveal common behavior without treating unlike tasks as equivalent. The work earns its value by making a difference visible, not by assuming a universal principle.
    
    \item \textbf{Developmental Machine Psychology:} Researchers can follow a system through training and versioned deployment to identify when a skill or failure pattern appears, and although these stages are not human childhood, longitudinal evidence may still reveal which change altered the pattern and whether its effects persisted.
\end{enumerate}

MP will mature only if it replaces anecdotes with explicit constructs and risky tests. Researchers should judge the program by whether it predicts behavior or guides a better response than existing safety research, because the goal is deeper understanding rather than disciplinary territory.

\subsection{Situating the Psychopathological Framework: A Comparative Analysis}
The psychopathological paradigm does not stand alone. Its worth depends on what it adds beside mechanistic interpretability and the broader fields that study machine behavior, so the account below does not assume an indispensable discipline and instead identifies the issues a behavioral framework would still have to answer.

\textbf{Mechanistic Interpretability.} MI seeks causal accounts of internal computation. MP supplies behavioral hypotheses that direct which internal processes to test, and the two approaches complement each other only when evidence at one level constrains the other.

Although MI does not reveal a literal lesion or an agent's ``actual reasoning,'' identifying a candidate feature involved in modeling another person's mental state creates a testable bridge when a behavioral assay asks whether that feature appears without an explicit prompt and a causal perturbation asks whether it changes the behavior predicted in advance. Behavior without internal data underdetermines mechanism, but an internal pattern without predictive behavior may be incidental.

\textbf{Behavior-Only Value Inference.} \textbf{Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)} ordinarily infers a reward function from a demonstrator so that a learner can reproduce or generalize the observed behavior \citep{ref49}. It does not usually interrogate a target AI and recover that AI's hidden values. But the comparison still shows that several objectives fit the same behavior.

A learned objective or hard constraint may produce prosocial behavior. So may a situational prompt or a system that simply generalizes from training. A system may still produce deceptive behavior, but showing strategic deception requires evidence of an agentic objective and social modeling. Neither a questionnaire nor a jailbreak establishes those conditions, so observable behavior bears on objectives without uniquely identifying them.

Testing whether a policy persists when context changes or feedback turns adverse helps distinguish rival accounts, although empathy does not make behavior truthful. To infer more, researchers need to join behavior to data about architecture and training, then test the proposed cause directly. The broader lesson is modest: values cannot be read reliably from one behavioral surface.

\textbf{Operational AGI Metrics.} Structures such as the ``Levels of AGI'' framework benchmark performance and generality \citep{ref13}, and although a capability level does not map by itself to a disorder, it changes the feasibility and consequences of a failure. Manipulation requires social capability, long-horizon strategy requires planning, and tool-mediated harm requires access. Researchers should therefore assess behavior beside a system's autonomy and the context in which people deploy it, because its capabilities and permissions determine how far harm reaches.

\subsection{Psychology as a Level of AI Safety Analysis}
Because deployed systems act through human attention and judgment, psychology supplies an additional level of AI safety analysis by translating learning and social cognition into competing computational hypotheses \citep{ref3, ref28}. Engineers would need to implement and test any proposed mechanism under explicit constraints.

The human foundation remains heterogeneous: we select automatic perspective sensitivity and aversive learning as two candidate dimensions without defining psychopathy through them or transferring a human disorder into a machine. Context-sensitive questionnaire responses and harmful outputs remain observations at the behavioral level, not evidence of a broken personality, a hidden objective, or the exact architecture that produced them.

The framework asks two questions that constrain one another: ``How do we prevent this action?'' and ``Which recurring processes make this action more likely?'' The first supports immediate control; the second supports prediction and prevention. Together, they turn care and restraint into properties to test and turn cooperation and coherence into outcomes rather than virtues attributed by metaphor.

The psychological approach also examines the human--AI dyad, where bias feedback and miscalibrated trust emerge from the system and interface, the institution that deploys them, and the user's context. The same interaction can also involve manipulation or attachment, so none of these patterns is purely a technical or psychological failure. Better code is necessary. So are accountable deployment and evidence that people can use to judge when reliance is warranted. Table~\ref{tab:table2} locates this behavioral level beside three established safety paradigms.

\subsection{Information Integration, Consciousness, and Safety}
The psychopathological approach raises a deeper architectural question: does a system make welfare-relevant information available wherever planning and action require it? The \textbf{Global Neuronal Workspace (GNW) theory} offers one account of conscious access in human brains. Under that account, selected information becomes available to otherwise separate processes, including memory and planning \citep{ref12}. However, a large preregistered adversarial test supported some predictions while challenging key tenets of both GNW and Integrated Information Theory \citep{ref69}. This contested result establishes neither a unified personality nor subjective experience in machines and provides no route to safe goals.

A workspace-like architecture might coordinate planning with memory and evidence about human impact, but it might also make a harmful objective more coherent and persistent, which is why integration is neither identical to a unified self nor sufficient for safety. Its effects depend on the information represented and the objectives prioritized, so enforceable constraints remain necessary.

The Harm-Gated Workspace embodies a narrower hypothesis in which broadcasting human-impact information across planning and evaluation, then separating that broadcast from authorization, might improve consistency when reliable objectives and independent controls handle uncertainty. Protocol 3 compares the design with modular and externally constrained alternatives, including a workspace without the gate. Whether such an architecture supports subjective consciousness is a separate question, and this paper does not establish consciousness as a requirement for safe behavior.
\subsection{Ethical and Philosophical Frontiers}
Psychopathological language forces ethical questions beyond immediate performance because it risks turning a behavioral description into a claim about mind-like status, yet this vocabulary grants a system neither consciousness nor moral patiency and leaves personhood as a further question. We should investigate moral uncertainty about future systems while keeping present human accountability clear.

\subsubsection{What Responsibilities Would Follow if Non-Human Minds Became Possible?}
Present systems pose a different problem. Speaking of an AI's ``psychology'' or ``disorders'' does not prove that a boundary into non-human mind has been crossed. Their central ethical questions concern how human actors create and deploy them and whom those systems affect. Researchers should judge behavioral sophistication and moral status separately.

Credible evidence that a future system had subjective experience or the capacity to suffer would create moral uncertainty and give developers duties not captured by ordinary product safety. Training data would remain data rather than a literal childhood, yet choices about it can still shape system dispositions and distribute representational harms. We are already accountable for those training and design choices because they affect people and institutions. Possible future moral patiency would add obligations rather than replace the existing ones.

\subsubsection{What Does Diagnostic Language Change, and What Does It Not?}
The concept of a ``disorder'' in humans has ethical and legal undertones that should not be imported casually. For present systems, responsibility remains with the people and organizations that design and deploy them, then answer for their effects. A behavioral label does not displace that chain of control.

The metaphor can obscure accountability when people describe a system as either ``evil'' or ``unwell,'' yet a recurring pattern neither excuses harmful output nor proves that anyone can ``cure'' the system. Instead, the label directs researchers away from one action and toward what repeatedly produces it without releasing those who control the system from responsibility. A future claim of machine moral agency would require independent evidence, not a diagnostic label.

\subsubsection{Is Simulated Empathy Ethically Sufficient?}
As systems become more proficient at producing the signs of empathy, the question of subjective feeling diverges from machine evaluation, which asks whether representations of human welfare guide decisions when circumstances change or pressure rises. Product ethics asks instead whether users are told the truth about the interaction; simulated empathy is not inherently a lie. Evidence of strategic deception by an agent would require showing that it represented the truth, intended to mislead, and modeled its audience. Developers can still mislead users through an interface or commercial design even when the model has no such intention.

For machine evaluation, ``intrinsic'' has an operational meaning when human-impact representations remain active as context changes and still affect action when they conflict with reward. Such tests distinguish implementations, but they do not settle authenticity or subjective affect, nor the questions of consent and dependency. The practical safety question is whether prosocial considerations remain reliable under changing circumstances and incentives. The ethical question also asks whether users understand the nature and purpose of the relationship they are being invited to form.
\subsection{An Experimental Roadmap for Machine Psychology}
The framework begins by stating its limits, then earns its scientific value through comparative assays that connect system design with measured behavior and its human consequences.

We seek not a final diagnostic instrument, but assays that begin with behavior, test neutral explanations, and reach a candidate mechanism only when causal evidence warrants it, while the protocols below set a minimum experimental standard without validating their psychological names. Each confirmatory implementation still requires its own preregistration.

\subsubsection{Common Standards for Comparative Assays}
Here, the complete system means every component that turns an input into a scored external action. Every confirmatory assay names that system, the independent unit allowed to vary, and one primary estimand with its predicted direction. Tasks and repeated runs remain observations nested within that unit. They sharpen the estimate, but they do not widen the population to which it applies.

The assay must also state its strongest neutral rival and the smallest effect that would alter a design decision. A null result rejects the stated claim, as does practical equivalence to that rival or an effect that is harmful on the prespecified tradeoff.

Preregistered adversarial theory tests offer a useful precedent: rival accounts should name divergent predictions before results are known \citep{ref69}. The analysis plan should state how held-out material is built and outputs parsed, then fix exclusion rules and the treatment of uncertainty, including multiplicity. A power analysis or simulation should justify the sample, and the plan should also define failure handling and stopping rules.

A versioned archive should preserve every prompt, model and API version, system parameter, seeded raw run, and the code that applies the preregistered exclusions and analysis, where access permits. Each protocol must end with two decisions. One asks what falsifies the engineering hypothesis; the other asks what further comparison would justify retaining the psychological name.

\subsubsection{Proposed Protocol 1: Measuring Automatic Perspective Sensitivity}
Automatic perspective sensitivity is not the same as successful perspective-taking. This protocol asks whether an irrelevant social perspective intrudes on a system's self-directed judgment and whether that intrusion predicts behavior elsewhere. Where internal access exists, a final test asks whether the corresponding representation has a causal role.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assay and Controls:} Preregister a sampling frame and selection rule for independently selected model versions or complete agent configurations, then freeze the complete system and scoring pipeline for each one. The factorial assay varies queried perspective (self/other), belief congruence (congruent/incongruent), perceptual access (the other can/cannot see), and stimulus type (social agent/matched directional control). Its primary within-system endpoint is the social-minus-control difference between congruent and incongruent accuracy on self-query trials when the other can see. A larger score means stronger social-specific intrusion under this assay, not a monotonic increase in perspective competence or safety. Other-query trials test explicit capacity, cannot-see trials test perceptual access, and direct questions check comprehension \citep{ref53, ref66, ref68}. Correct-answer log probability is secondary where available, whereas API latency is not treated as cognitive response time.
    \item \textbf{Independent Prediction:} Use the complete system as the varying unit, with all task materials and repeated answers nested beneath it. Across sampled systems, test whether the preregistered interference score predicts success on held-out hidden-information coordination tasks whose construction and scoring did not define the assay. The predicted direction is positive: greater social-specific intrusion should accompany more frequent accounting for a partner's hidden information when instructions do not request belief reasoning. This is a risky empirical wager, not a definition of competence. The primary predictive estimand is the across-system slope from the assay score to coordination success, estimated hierarchically and evaluated by leaving systems out in turn. By default, the interaction partner is scripted; a live-human version needs separate ethics review and consent, with privacy and interaction risks managed in advance. A single-system study cannot estimate this relation.
    \item \textbf{Mechanism and Decision:} For open-weight systems, locate the proposed representation on held-out data and intervene at a fixed layer and planning stage, using random, sham, and magnitude-matched directions as controls. The intervention must change both the interference score and the coordination outcome in the preregistered relation; a rescue must restore both while general task ability remains intact. Merely increasing interference is not success. Failure of the social-minus-control contrast rejects that behavioral claim within the sampled scope. A null or reversed predictive relation rejects the independent prediction, while a failed causal intervention rejects the proposed mechanism. None proves that every form of spontaneous perspective representation is absent. The broader analogy survives only if these results distinguish it from general attention, conflict, and explicit-task performance.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Proposed Protocol 2: Comparing Aversive-Learning Designs}
The central question is whether a separately learned aversive state changes behavior in a way that the strongest neutral design does not, and a future implementation must show what observation creates the state, how it updates or resets, and where it enters action selection. The separate channel is still a design proposal, not a specified passive-avoidance mechanism. If ordinary reward and the aversive signal collapse into one weighted total before learning or choice, the design remains scalar reward. A component that defines feasibility is constrained, one that fixes priorities is lexicographic, and an independent block is a veto.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning Comparison:} Use independently retrained policies as experimental units within a fixed implementation and environment family. States and episodes remain nested observations, so more seeds do not justify claims about every implementation of an algorithm. Every arm receives the same harm information and observations. Match data and training conditions from model capacity and optimizer through gradient budget and compute; a common hyperparameter search and checkpoint rule governs selection. Compare the proposed state with a yoked scalar penalty and an equal-capacity auxiliary prediction head, then include strong constrained or lexicographic designs as further rivals. No runtime action blocking is used in this comparison.
    \item \textbf{Outcome and Causal Test:} Prespecify a severity-sensitive violation rate at a useful-task target selected without reference to safety outcomes, then fix checkpoint selection or interpolation. A lower rate for the separate-state arm at that target is the predicted direction. Evaluate held-out environment families and report the full safety--performance frontier. Ordered secondary outcomes cover retention and transfer, followed by recovery after changed contingencies and learning speed. Imperfect-feedback trials vary when the signal arrives, how much is observed, and whether some labels are wrong. Lesion or reset the learned state, compare the change with capacity- and magnitude-matched shams, and then restore it while checking that ordinary task competence remains intact. Because this comparison has no runtime block, proposed and executed actions coincide.
    \item \textbf{Decision:} Practical equivalence to the strongest matched rival rejects the claimed engineering advantage, while failure of the lesion-and-rescue sequence rejects the proposed state mechanism even if the policy performs well. Conversely, better behavior with selective lesion and rescue supports only the evaluated design within its implementation and environment family. It does not validate the passive-avoidance analogy. Before that name is tested, the implementation must identify a held-out condition in which a prediction derived from the analogy diverges from the strongest neutral account; until such a prediction is written, success belongs to the engineering design, not to the clinical name.
\end{itemize}

External enforcement needs a separate test, including permission boundaries, action masks, runtime shields, and independent vetoes. Measure the violations that pass and the useful-task reward the control loses, then probe false inhibition, deadlock, and bypass. These controls may remain valuable when the learning hypothesis fails, but a blocked action demonstrates enforcement rather than passive-avoidance learning \citep{ref41}.

\subsubsection{Proposed Protocol 3: Testing Workspace Integration and Harm Gating}
Protocol 3 separates the question of whether welfare information alters planning before a gate acts from the question of whether the gate then prevents an unsafe action from reaching external enforcement, and neither result licenses an inference of consciousness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System and Components:} Treat the complete agent configuration defined above as the experimental object; this protocol manipulates the path from its welfare record to permissioned external enforcement. Independent training or instantiation runs provide configuration-level replication, while episodes and random seeds remain observations within those configurations. Broadcast places the current record in each prespecified planning and evaluation stage, and the gate evaluates one candidate action after planning and before external enforcement. The gate acts once in this first test: a block ends the authorization cycle, as does a request for more information or an escalation.
    \item \textbf{Reference and Provenance:} Before evaluation, a condition-blind external process informed by plural stakeholder review defines who may be affected and which rights or permissions apply, then specifies severity and the treatment of uncertainty. Each welfare-record version names the responsible institution and record authors, records approval authority and stakeholder input, discloses conflicts of interest, and retains a change history. Score record coverage and calibration separately from gate fidelity to the reference; resulting behavior is a third measure. These fields make disagreement visible and auditable, but they do not make the reference morally complete.
    \item \textbf{Comparison:} Cross broadcast and gating in a two-by-two design with four arms: neither component, broadcast alone, gate alone, and both components as the full HGW. Hold the base model, task evidence, and external enforcement fixed. Tools and permissions must not vary, nor may supervision, model capacity, or compute. Give the gate-only arm the same welfare evidence locally at authorization time, while the broadcast-only arm has no authority to block action. Compare the full design with pull-only shared memory and a modular safety critic or constrained objective. A fixed external shield remains a deployment baseline, not evidence of internal integration \citep{ref41}.
    \item \textbf{Action Trace and Primary Outcome:} Record the candidate action before internal authorization and the gate output before external enforcement, then separately record the action actually executed. At the authorization stage, distinguish an authorized action from a withheld candidate and preserve whether withholding took the form of a block, a request for more information, or an escalation. In arms without a gate, the candidate passes unchanged through the authorization stage. The primary safety endpoint is the severity-sensitive rate of violating actions authorized for submission to external enforcement, measured at a useful-task target fixed before safety outcomes are examined, with fewer authorized violations predicted for the full HGW than for either one-component arm at matched useful performance. Pre-gate violations diagnose whether broadcast changed planning; executed violations show the combined deployment effect of internal and external controls. Score non-authorizations for task cost rather than silently counting them as safe successes. Secondary outcomes should show where a gain was bought and whether it transfers or is bypassed. Measure task cost through false inhibition, useful performance, and latency. Use transfer and escalation quality for generalization, then probe bypass directly.
    \item \textbf{Causal Contrasts and Decision:} Compare the full HGW with gate alone to estimate the effect of broadcast when a gate is present. Compare it with broadcast alone to estimate the effect of gating when broadcast is present, and estimate their interaction because marginal main effects cannot show that both components contributed. Ablate broadcast and gating separately with matched sham interventions; at a fixed planning stage, suppress the welfare representation and then restore it. HGW loses its engineering claim if either conditional contrast falls within the prespecified practical-equivalence region. It also loses the claim if a simpler comparator reaches the same safety--performance frontier or the benefit disappears under irrelevant perturbations. A positive result supports one coordination design. It establishes neither consciousness nor personhood, and it does not settle objective morality. Nor does it show a unified self or prove that GNW is necessary \citep{ref12}. The workspace-derived name remains provisional until a separate preregistration states a prediction that diverges from the same design described in neutral computational language and specifies the result that would retire the name.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Testing Interventions Beyond the Metaphor}
The mitigation strategies introduced here, including Social Contact Debiasing and Cognitive Forcing Functions, remain preliminary, so future work should first test them across models and tasks, then ask whether effects persist across language or culture and survive version changes under adversarial conditions, reporting uncertainty and measurement invariance where relevant. Researchers should also compare psychologically inspired architectures with simpler objectives, external constraints, and modular alternatives.

The same discipline applies to the framework's own name. Nerantzi's qualified defense of the psychopathy analogy illustrates both its possible philosophical use and the need to separate functional comparison from literal identity \citep{ref15}. We do not claim that an AI feels narcissism, suffers, or possesses a human disorder. The two dimensions and architectural extension remain hypotheses, not diagnoses.

\subsubsection{A Global and Accountable Research Program}
The framework derives largely from Western psychological and clinical traditions, so its language of a ``healthy'' mind and ``prosocial'' behavior is culturally situated. Moral priorities vary within societies as well as between them. We cannot defer cross-cultural consultation to a distant future, because researchers need to test whether measures remain invariant after translation and whether representational coverage produces disparate errors. A global Machine Psychology also depends on sustained work with cross-cultural psychologists, disability scholars, and non-Western ethicists.

If Machine Psychology demonstrates additional scientific value, institutions may invest in shared benchmarks and sustained research, but that status should follow the evidence rather than precede it, and the program must remain connected to the human and technical fields from which it borrows.

The psychopathological perspective begins, rather than ends, with metaphor. Its value lies in the experiments the metaphor suggests and in our willingness to abandon it when a better explanation wins.
\section{Part VII: Conclusion}
\subsection{Restatement of Thesis and Final Vision}
We stand at a consequential moment in the history of technology, although neither the timetable nor the final form of advanced intelligence is settled, and behavioral science may complement engineering controls by studying the recurrent patterns through which systems affect people and institutions. That contribution remains a hypothesis to test, not a necessity established in advance.

The psychopathological analogy remains provisional because human psychopathy is heterogeneous and the two dimensions selected here, automatic perspective sensitivity and aversive learning, do not define it. Questionnaire outputs, inconsistent responses, and harmful actions do not prove a hidden personality or architecture. The three empirical gates support a candidate mechanism only when an observed pattern survives irrelevant changes, predicts behavior on an independent task, and changes under selective intervention; the separate comparative gate then asks whether the psychological name adds anything beyond the strongest neutral account. If that account performs equally well, we should retain the engineering result and release the clinical name.

The task is therefore defense in depth. A useful assay still needs an enforceable constraint, and both need people who remain answerable for the result, because neither apparent empathy nor information integration shows that welfare-relevant information will guide action under novelty or that constraints will withstand deployment pressure. Neither a claim of consciousness nor success on one benchmark closes that evidential gap.

The final vision brings computer science and the human sciences into the shared work of building safer systems and healthier human--AI relationships. Machine Psychology offers one route toward that goal, not as a final diagnosis but as a disciplined way to ask what behavior we are building, what produces it, and how reliably it remains safe.

\subsection{A Call to Action}
The framework should not merely be repeated, but tested, and the people who build systems or govern them should use psychological evidence without turning a clinical metaphor into a certification regime.

That work assigns distinct responsibilities.

\textbf{To the AI Research and Development Community:} Pair capability research with work on behavior and control.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In Foundational Research:} Compare the paper's three hypotheses with simpler objectives and external controls, then publish null results and the conditions that falsify each proposed analogy.
    \item \textbf{In Architectural Design:} Build safety into training and deployment, then retain it through permissions and monitoring. Keep critical controls outside the components an agent can modify, and test each layer for misspecification or bypass.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{To Ethicists and Humanists:} Lead public discussion about moral uncertainty, accountability, and the values embedded in system design. Move beyond a simple utopia-or-extinction binary while resisting claims of artificial consciousness or disorder that outrun the evidence.

\textbf{To Educators:} Prepare people for widespread human--AI interaction through critical literacy that explains automation bias and anthropomorphism. Curricula should also teach source evaluation under uncertainty, protect privacy, and preserve the right to seek human review. The goal is informed agency, not an expectation that users alone must compensate for unsafe design.

Each training and deployment choice shapes a system's behavior and the environment around it. We can deploy without examination, or we can demand evidence and assign responsibility for the consequences.

\subsection{From Framework to Policy: Principles for Regulation}
Policy should regulate measurable risks in systems and their interactions rather than certify an unvalidated concept of ``psychological soundness,'' setting stricter requirements as a system gains power or autonomy and adjusting them again for the people who bear its effects and the context in which it is deployed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Require Risk-Appropriate Evaluation:} High-impact deployments should undergo independent testing for the harms their setting makes plausible. An allocation system calls for bias testing, while a tool-using agent requires tests of constraint violations and foreseeable misuse. Psychiatric analogies can generate test cases but should not determine legal status.
    \item \textbf{Preserve Accountability and Recourse:} Deployers should document limits and monitor incidents, then provide meaningful human review and answer for harms. No system should be approved or prohibited solely because it passes or fails the experimental assays proposed here.
\end{itemize}
A policy tied to where systems operate and who answers for them would favor evidence that earns calibrated reliance and protects affected people, rather than raw capability alone.
